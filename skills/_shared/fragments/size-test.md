**The size test.** A part of the design earns its place by naming what breaks without it: a part of the measured problem, or a structural property of what is built now (the design standard's last paragraph). "It doesn't cover every case" is not that unless the uncovered case is costly; "another component needs it" is not that either — follow the chain to its root and judge the root against the problem size. The test runs twice.

**When you offer choices about how much to build**, one option is the smallest change — down to "do it by hand" or "leave it" — priced in the terms of the problem size, and recommending anything larger needs evidence that it fails the test. An option that drags in follow-on components or decisions names them ("(b) brings a workflow, a selector and a label protocol"): they are part of its price, and the user never sees them one by one. The do-least option is one choice *inside* a question, not a reason to skip it and not a verdict: ask every question only the user can answer that would change the design — who it is for, what is at stake, which constraint is real, which of two live patterns wins. What the user stated as a requirement is a given: price it, never relitigate it, and challenge it only with a question ("is X enough, or do you need Y?"), never by quietly deciding for them.

**Before the design goes to the user for its summary or final approval** — the subtraction pass — apply the test to every decision and component. "Nothing breaks" is a claim of fact: check it like one and cite it, or keep the item; where it holds, propose the cut. When a chain's root only saves what the do-least option covers cheaply, show the chain with its total price and ask once whether to keep it, even if the user approved the root earlier. Show the cut list with the design, so the user can restore anything they want back; what stays cut goes to Out of Scope through `to-issues`, with the reason. Alternatives never proposed are not cuts — they go straight to Out of Scope.
